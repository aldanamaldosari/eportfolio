Initial post — agent communication languages versus method invocation

Unit 5

Agent communication languages (ACLs), such as KQML, rest on a different premise than method invocation in Java or Python. When an object invokes a method, it directly executes another object's code: the caller decides. In agent systems, communication is an action performed to influence the receiver, which retains autonomy over whether and how to respond (Wooldridge, 2009). This is grounded in speech act theory, which treats utterances as performatives — acts such as requesting or informing that change the world (Searle, 1969). KQML operationalises this by wrapping content in performatives like ask-if and tell, separating communicative intent from content (Finin et al., 1994).

The advantages follow from this design. It preserves autonomy: an agent may refuse or negotiate rather than simply execute. It enables loose coupling, since the performative, content language, and ontology are separated, allowing heterogeneous agents built by different organisations to interoperate without shared code — impossible with method invocation, which requires compile-time knowledge of interfaces. Facilitator services (advertise, broker) further support open systems where agents join and leave at runtime (Finin et al., 1994).

The disadvantages are equally clear. KQML historically lacked formal semantics, so implementations interpreted performatives inconsistently, undermining the interoperability it promised (Labrou, Finin and Peng, 1999). Method invocation, by contrast, has precise, verifiable semantics. Moreover, ACLs defer the hardest problem: meaningful communication still requires a shared ontology, and in open systems agents hold asymmetric, incomplete conceptualisations, forcing costly runtime negotiation of ontological correspondences (Payne and Tamma, 2014).

In short, the two are tools for different regimes: method invocation suits closed, trusted systems; ACLs are justified when autonomy, heterogeneity, and openness make direct invocation impossible — at the price of semantic ambiguity and ontology alignment.

I agree with your distinction between agent communication languages and conventional method invocation, particularly your point that autonomy changes the nature of interaction. In method invocation, behaviour is determined through a predefined interface, whereas an autonomous agent retains control over whether and how it responds. Your discussion of loose coupling is also important in heterogeneous multi-agent systems, where independently developed agents may need to communicate without sharing implementation-level interfaces. Bagherzadeh and Arun-Kumar (2006), for example, demonstrate how FIPA-ACL communication can be incorporated into a flexible agent programming framework in which communicative acts and their semantics can be defined independently of conventional procedure calls.

I also agree with your concern that this flexibility introduces semantic challenges. Singh (1998) argues that agent interoperability remains problematic when communication semantics depend heavily on representations internal to individual agents, proposing greater emphasis on observable social interaction instead. This complements your point that adopting a common ACL does not necessarily guarantee that independently developed agents will interpret communication consistently.

Your observation about shared ontologies therefore remains particularly important. Even if agents recognise the same communicative structure, meaningful exchange still depends on sufficient agreement about the concepts being represented. As a useful extension, Verdicchio and Colombetti (2004) propose modelling communicative acts through social commitments between agents. This can make the consequences of communication more explicit, but it does not remove the ontology-alignment problem you identify, since agents may still conceptualise the communicated content differently.

Overall, I agree that ACLs are better suited to open, heterogeneous and autonomous environments, while their effectiveness still depends on resolving semantic and conceptual differences between participating agents.

Cited: Bagherzadeh and Arun-Kumar (2006); Singh (1998); Verdicchio and Colombetti (2004).

Summary post

Unit 7

My first post argued that agent communication languages such as KQML and method invocation in Java or Python are not competing mechanisms but tools for different regimes. Method invocation offers precise and verifiable semantics for closed, trusted systems; ACLs, based on speech act theory (Searle, 1969) and implemented via performatives such as ask-if and tell (Finin et al., 1994), preserve agent autonomy and allow for loose coupling among heterogeneous agents. I identified two main costs: KQML's weak formal semantics and the deferred ontology alignment, which in open systems has to be negotiated at runtime when the system is asymmetric and incomplete (Payne and Tamma, 2014).

The conversation made this point stronger in two ways. First, replies by Singh (1998) sharpened my semantic critique: the deeper problem is not that KQML's performatives were loosely defined, but that mentalistic semantics — meaning in terms of what an agent believes and what it wants to do — is not easily discernible because no agent can view another's psychological state. Solutions based on observable social commitments (Verdicchio and Colombetti, 2004) respond to that issue. Second, the conversations in the discussion revealed that this semantic problem and the ontology-alignment problem are not in conflict but complementary: one is about what a message binds an agent to, and the other is about whether the two sides understand what it means.

My overall conclusion stands, but with greater precision: the engineering case for ACLs rests on autonomy, heterogeneity, and openness, and their practical effectiveness depends on progress in verifiable semantics and runtime ontology negotiation — both active research areas rather than solved problems.

I.S.

Unit 6 · on performatives, loose coupling and the cost of abstraction

Their post argued that performatives express intent rather than dictate execution, giving loose coupling between independently built agents, and weighed that flexibility against its semantic and architectural overhead.

The line I keep coming back to is your closing one, that the flexibility of ACLs has to be justified by the requirements of the system. I agree, and I think it raises an awkward question for the autonomy argument earlier in your post. On paper, a receiving agent can decide how, or whether, to respond. Once a system is deployed, that freedom shrinks fast. The Contract Net protocol spells out which responses are acceptable at each stage of a negotiation (Smith, 1980), and FIPA interaction protocols do much the same with their reply sequences. An agent that keeps refusing or ignoring requests has, for practical purposes, stopped participating. Wooldridge (2009) says something similar when he notes that autonomy always operates within the social conventions of the wider system.

If that holds, the overhead you describe looks harder to justify. Where protocols have already reduced interaction to fairly predictable sequences, a full ACL adds semantic and architectural cost without giving you much more than a carefully specified service interface would. My suspicion is that the real criterion is organisational. ACLs earn their keep when the agents belong to independent parties; under single ownership, they rarely do. Would you apply that test once for the whole system, or separately for each type of interaction?

Peer response 2

F.O.

Unit 6 · on KQML's structure, expressiveness and verifiability

Their post set out KQML through speech act theory, claiming expressiveness, interoperability and autonomy, against the costs of ontology matching and unverifiable intent.

This is a clear and well-organised post, and I like that you name specific performatives rather than describing KQML in the abstract. One small point on structure first: KIF was a companion to KQML from the Knowledge Sharing Effort rather than a required part of it. KQML is deliberately agnostic about the content language, so KIF is one option among several, and in practice implementations used Prolog, Lisp expressions and other formats (Finin et al., 1994). That flexibility is part of why the language spread, but it also fed the deeper problem I want to raise.

Your advantage of semantic expressiveness rests on agents interpreting performatives the same way, and this is exactly where KQML struggled. The original specification described what performatives meant informally, without formal semantics, so different implementations attached different meanings to the same performative and sometimes invented incompatible new ones (Labrou and Finin, 1997). A tell from one system was not always a tell in another. Expressiveness without agreed semantics can therefore give the appearance of shared understanding rather than the substance of it, which is worth keeping in mind when we assess how much the performative layer really buys us. Do you think FIPA-ACL's attempt at formal semantics solved this, or does the fact that its semantics rely on unverifiable mental states leave the problem in place?
