Collaborative Discussion 1: Industry 4.0 to Industry 5.0

Units 1–3 — the shift from a techno-economic to a socio-technical view of industry, tested against real system failures.

A cohort discussion on the move from Industry 4.0 to Industry 5.0 — from a techno-economic focus on automation and connected systems to one centred on human-centricity, sustainability and resilience — argued through real failures in each of our own sectors.

Components

  • Initial post — respond to the question. Unit 1.
  • Peer responses — respond to at least two peers' posts. Unit 2.
  • Summary post — drawing on the initial post, the feedback received and the content of the three units. Unit 3.

The cases in the thread

  • Optus, 2023 — mine. A nationwide outage in which automated safeguards protected the routers and left millions without connection, emergency callers among them.
  • Robodebt — P.M.'s. Averaged annual income used to judge fortnightly welfare entitlements, raising debts that did not exist.
  • Toyota, 2023 — F.B.'s. A parts-ordering server out of disk space during maintenance, with an identically configured backup that failed the same way.

Initial post

Unit 1

My initial post argued that the 2023 Optus outage was a failure of resilience rather than of technology: automated safeguards protected the routers but left millions of people, including callers to emergency services, without connection (ACMA, 2024). The discussion with my peers has widened that view.

The two incidents I responded to followed the same pattern. At Toyota, a server ran out of disk space during routine maintenance, and an identically configured backup failed in the same way. In Robodebt, averaged annual income was used to judge fortnightly entitlements, producing debts that did not exist. In all three cases, the systems behaved as designed. The failure lay in assumptions nobody had tested: default router limits, a backup with no real independence, and data that did not fit its purpose. This supports Metcalf et al.'s (2024) view that Industry 4.0's techno-economic focus needs Industry 5.0's emphasis on human-centricity and resilience.

The units sharpened the machine learning lesson. Géron (2025) shows that a model is only as reliable as its data, and the exploratory data analysis covered in Unit 2 is where such assumptions should be examined before anything is deployed (Patil, 2018). Unit 3's distinction between correlation and causation (Leetaru, 2019) explains Robodebt well: an income mismatch suggested an overpayment but could never prove one.

I have therefore refined my position. My initial post stressed human oversight after failure. I now see that resilience starts earlier, with validating data and design assumptions, and continues with monitoring the errors that harm people, not only the savings systems produce. For my own work in government telecommunications, this means treating every automated safeguard and dataset as an assumption to test, not a guarantee.

Cited: ACMA (2024); Géron (2025); Leetaru (2019); Metcalf, Nousala and Ing (2024); Patil (2018).

Responses I wrote to peers in Unit 2.

Peer response 1

P.M.

Unit 2 · on Robodebt and automated decisions in public administration

Their post read Australia's Robodebt scheme through the Industry 5.0 lens: welfare records matched against averaged tax data could not establish earnings in the fortnights used to assess entitlement, and the burden of challenging the result fell on the recipient. They argued it was a failure of the wider information system — data, procedures and accountability — rather than of software, and asked how public organisations should measure automation's success when savings and citizens' welfare conflict.

Your point that data suitable for one purpose may be unsuitable for another goes to the heart of Robodebt, and I think it also shows where the harm could have been stopped.

The first opportunity came before deployment. Géron (2025) warns that poor-quality or unrepresentative data undermines a system, however sound its logic. A small pilot comparing average-income debts with the actual payslips of a sample of recipients would have exposed the gap between annual and fortnightly earnings before a single notice went out.

The second concerns how the data was interpreted. Leetaru (2019) reminds us that data-driven systems detect correlations, not causes. A mismatch between tax records and reported income may suggest an overpayment, but it cannot prove a debt exists. Had a human verified each case before a notice was issued, the burden of proof would have stayed with the government rather than shifting to citizens.

This also offers an answer to your question about measuring success. Novaković et al. (2017) show that an overall performance figure can hide very different kinds of error. In Robodebt, the costly error was the false positive: a debt raised against someone who owed nothing. If the department had reported how often notices were overturned on review alongside the money recovered, the harm would have been visible early. And as Géron (2025) argues, live systems need monitoring that raises alerts when performance falls; a rising overturn rate could have given your human oversight a clear trigger to suspend the process.

Should agencies publish these error rates, so that citizens can hold automated decisions to account?

Cited: Géron (2025); Leetaru (2019); Novaković et al. (2017).

Peer response 2

F.B.

Unit 2 · on the Toyota production stoppage and just-in-time supply

Writing from procurement at a carmaker, their post took the August 2023 Toyota stoppage as a case of digital dependence rather than attack: a parts-ordering server ran out of disk space during routine maintenance and the backup, identically configured, failed the same way, halting all fourteen Japanese assembly plants for about a day. Their conclusion was that a backup failing in the same way is no backup, and that just-in-time needs a practised manual fallback.

Your post shows clearly that the weakness was in the design, not in an attacker. I would add that each failure you describe could have been prevented with a specific measure.

Predicting the failure. Running out of disk space is predictable, because storage use grows over time. The predictive-maintenance methods surveyed by Drakaki et al. (2022) learn from condition data to anticipate faults before they happen. The same approach, applied to server telemetry instead of motors, could have forecast that the disk would fill up. Zhou et al. (2023) argue that machine learning in Industry 4.0 should support exactly this kind of reliable operation, not only efficiency.

Real redundancy. You are right that a backup which fails in the same way is not a backup. Both servers had the same configuration and the same capacity, so the same fault took out both. A backup built differently, or one checked for spare capacity before maintenance began, would have broken that shared dependency.

Monitoring and rollback. Géron (2025) stresses that a live system needs monitoring with automatic alerts, and backups that allow a quick rollback when a change goes wrong. A pre-maintenance check of free space on both servers, plus a tested rollback, would have caught the problem before production stopped.

Human fallback. Morales, Nousala and Ghobakhloo (2024) argue that Industry 4.0 left human-centricity gaps that Industry 5.0 must close. A rehearsed manual ordering process for critical parts is one practical way to close them, turning a full stop into a slowdown.

Do you think just-in-time can ever be truly resilient, or does Industry 5.0 call for keeping small buffer stocks for critical parts?

Cited: Drakaki et al. (2022); Géron (2025); Morales, Nousala and Ghobakhloo (2024); Zhou et al. (2023).
