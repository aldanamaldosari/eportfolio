Initial post — from recognition to generation

Unit 9 · 25 September 2026

The evolution of deep learning helps explain why its ethical implications now demand attention. Ten years ago, the biggest successes were discriminative ones; Microsoft's networks were able to outperform humans in image recognition (Thomsen, 2015). Today, transformer-based architectures underpin systems like DALL·E and ChatGPT that generate new content rather than classify content (Or, 2025), and I would argue that this move from recognition to generation makes the ethical problems structural rather than secondary. Generative systems require provenance and authorship questions, can create and amplify bias in their training data, and lower the cost of creating convincing misinformation (Radanliev et al., 2024). Izabayo (2026) tackles an even wider societal issue: how AI benefits are distributed across countries, which he argues is the least well-known aspect of national AI strategy globally. Ethical deployment is therefore not just a design problem but a governance one as well.

For the second task, I researched responsible deployment with a focus on privacy. Radanliev et al. (2024) argue that AI systems must handle large volumes of personal data, so privacy protection should be built into the system in advance through differential privacy and federated learning, and that developers should be responsible for monitoring bias and errors throughout the system's lifecycle. This is critical because deep learning models are opaque, so harm can go unnoticed without meaningful protection.

I would address this risk in my own agent system, which retrieves and curates academic literature, in three ways: minimising the personal data agents collect and store; verifying every retrieved source's metadata before it enters any output, so unverified content is discarded rather than repaired; and keeping a human reviewer over the final synthesis, as a reflection of the transparency and lifecycle-accountability principles Radanliev et al. (2024) identify as central to responsible deployment.

Thoroughly enjoyed reading your initial post, Aldana. Your point on verifying retrieved sources' metadata before it enters any output is critical for academic AI agents. As you correctly mentioned, unverified data is discarded, which is necessary since AI outputs can produce inaccurate references. What would you recommend if verification fails? This could happen if the article cited is correct, but the findings are misrepresented.

A human-in-the-loop review of the final synthesis aims to catch errors that may be difficult to detect. Another ethical issue is the growing concern about who is responsible when misleading or harmful information surfaces. There is a growing concern with "responsibility gaps", which can be further divided into the moral accountability gap, culpability gap, public accountability gap, and active responsibility gap (Santoni de Sio and Mecacci, 2021). From a governance perspective, this is a grey area that isn't yet universally aligned. What would your recommendation be, even on AI output error: who is responsible based on the respective gaps surfaced?

Lastly, transparency is critical when thinking about ethical issues around AI-generated material since it's becoming increasingly difficult to identify and differentiate between the two, which poses a risk of misinformation. Zhao et al. (2023) introduced a concept of provably robust watermarking for AI-generated text, which could be a universal solution for differentiating between original content and AI content. Should responsibility for clarifying AI content rest with the platforms where it's posted or with the tools that generate the output?

Cited: Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021); Zhao et al. (2023).

L.F.

30 September 2026

Your agent design turns abstract generative-AI ethics into concrete engineering decisions, and I would like to suggest some preventive measures that build on your three safeguards.

First, checking metadata confirms that a source exists, but not that it says what the output claims. Walters and Wilder (2023) found that 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated, and many of the real ones still contained substantive errors. This matters for Frederick's question about misrepresented findings. One answer is claim-level grounding: use retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020), then run an entailment check that links each sentence to a quoted passage. Any claim the retrieved text does not support gets flagged, not silently passed.

Second, a human reviewer only helps if they stay alert. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) show that people come to over-trust automation that is usually reliable, so a reviewer who approves many polished syntheses may stop checking closely. Showing the low-confidence claims first, and occasionally inserting known errors as a test, could keep the review meaningful.

Third, I would argue that the responsibility gaps Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) describe get smaller when accountability is built into the system. Versioned logs of prompts, retrieved sources, and reviewer decisions support the end-to-end auditing proposed by Raji et al. (2020), so it becomes possible to trace where a failure started.

Finally, watermarking is unlikely to be a universal solution, because Sadasivan et al. (2023) show that paraphrasing can remove it. The EU AI Act already requires providers to mark generative outputs (European Union, 2024, Art. 50). This suggests responsibility starts with the tool, and platforms act as a second layer.

Cited: European Union (2024); Lewis et al. (2020); Parasuraman and Manzey (2010); Raji et al. (2020); Sadasivan et al. (2023); Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021); Walters and Wilder (2023).

Summary post

Unit 11

My initial post argued that deep learning's shift from discriminative to generative systems makes its ethical issues structural. I also argued that responsible deployment requires privacy and accountability to be engineered in across the whole system lifecycle (Radanliev et al., 2024).

The discussion strengthened this position. Peer threads showed that generative models can amplify bias beyond real-world baselines (Sun et al., 2024). They also showed that these models can memorise sensitive training data, which can then resurface at inference (Zhang et al., 2024). Because bias enters at several stages (Gallegos et al., 2024), no single correction at build time is enough.

The responses to my own post exposed a gap in my safeguards. Frederick asked what happens when a cited source is real but its findings are misrepresented. Lana answered this directly: metadata verification confirms that a source exists, not that it supports the claim. Walters and Wilder (2023) found that even genuine generated citations often contained substantive errors. I would therefore move from source-level to claim-level checking, linking each sentence to a retrieved passage and flagging any claim the passage does not support. Lana also noted that reviewers become complacent with reliable automation (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010), so my reviewer should see low-confidence claims first.

On Frederick's question of responsibility, I now think the gaps Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) describe narrow when accountability is designed in. Versioned logs of prompts, sources and reviewer decisions make failures traceable (Raji et al., 2020). Some choices, however, remain value judgements that engineering teams cannot settle alone, such as which patterns a model should resist. This supports Izabayo's (2026) case for governance frameworks.

My agent system now has four safeguards: data minimisation, claim-level verification, an audited human review, and post-deployment monitoring for bias and leakage.

Unit 10 · on bias, copyright, memorisation, environmental cost and misuse

Their post surveyed five ethical dimensions of generative deep learning — algorithmic bias, copyright and authorship, memorisation of training data, environmental cost, and malicious use — and closed by calling for a holistic approach combining technical innovation with governance.

I particularly valued the point about memorisation in diffusion models (Zhang et al., 2024), which shows that privacy risk exists not only in how training data is collected but in what a deployed model can later reproduce. That distinction is often missed.

There is clear overlap with my own post: I also identified privacy as a central concern, drawing on Radanliev et al. (2024), who argue that protections such as differential privacy and federated learning must be engineered into systems from the start rather than added afterwards. Your memorisation example strengthens that argument, since it demonstrates why post-hoc safeguards are inadequate — by inference time, the sensitive data is already embedded in the model's weights.

One possible extension concerns your conclusion. You rightly call for governance alongside technical fixes, but the ethical issues you list are all system-level. Izabayo (2026) argues there is also a societal-scale dimension: how the economic gains of generative AI are distributed, which his comparative analysis of national AI strategies finds to be the most neglected pillar worldwide. Adding this would connect your governance point to a concrete policy gap. How do you think the environmental costs you cite (Luccioni et al., 2023) should be weighed against these economic considerations — and who should be responsible for that trade-off?

Peer response 2

L.K.

Unit 10 · on bias amplification and fairness as a multi-stage problem

Their post focused on bias alone, arguing that generative models intensify the inequalities in their training data rather than merely reflecting them, and that fairness must therefore be addressed across development rather than corrected at the end.

The Sun et al. (2024) audit is a particularly strong choice, because it moves the debate beyond "models mirror biased data" — showing DALL·E 2 exceeding the bias of both census statistics and Google Images demonstrates amplification, which is a materially different and more serious claim.

Your conclusion that fairness cannot be a final "correction" aligns closely with the argument in my own post. Drawing on Radanliev et al. (2024), I argued that responsible deployment requires safeguards to be engineered in from the start, with developers accountable for monitoring bias and errors across the system's entire lifecycle. Your multi-stage framing via Gallegos et al. (2024) and their lifecycle position reinforce each other: both reject the idea that ethics can be bolted on after the technical work is done.

On your closing question — which patterns to preserve and which to resist — I would suggest the decision cannot rest with developers alone. Gallegos et al.'s point that fairness is value-dependent implies it is partly a governance question, and Izabayo (2026) argues that such choices sit within wider national frameworks for responsible AI rather than inside engineering teams. A practical starting point might be distinguishing patterns that are merely statistical from those with documented discriminatory effect. Do you think a domain-specific standard, such as occupational representation matching real labour statistics, would be workable, or does Sun et al.'s comparison show even "real-world" baselines are contested?
