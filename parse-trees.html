The task

  • The government raised interest rates.
  • The internet gives everyone a voice.
  • The man saw the dog with the telescope.

The grammar

A parse tree makes the hierarchical structure of a sentence explicit, so that each syntactic unit corresponds to a subtree (Zimmerman, 2019). Two families of parse tree do this differently. A constituency tree, used here, groups words into phrases and labels each group by its category. A dependency tree — the form Zimmerman (2019) works with — takes the words themselves as nodes and labels the edges with the grammatical relations between them, radiating from the main verb. The activity asks for the first; the contrast matters later.

Every label used in the four trees is listed below, with an example drawn from the three sentences.

SymbolCategoryWhat it coversExample
SSentence The whole clause: a subject phrase plus a predicate phrase. The internet gives everyone a voice
NPNoun phrase A noun and the words that belong with it; fills subject and object positions. the government, a voice
VPVerb phrase The verb together with its objects and any phrases modifying the action. raised interest rates
PPPrepositional phrase A preposition plus the noun phrase it governs. with the telescope
DetDeterminer Specifies a noun — articles, demonstratives, quantifiers. the, a
NNoun A thing, person or concept. dog, rates
VVerb The action or state at the head of the verb phrase. saw, gives
PPreposition Relates a noun phrase to the rest of the clause. with
ProPronoun Stands in for a noun phrase on its own. everyone

Production rules

The trees are derived from one small context-free grammar. Phrase-structure rules build the branches; lexical rules attach the words.

Phrase structure
  S   → NP VP
  NP  → Det N | N N | Pro | NP PP
  VP  → V NP | V NP NP | V NP PP
  PP  → P NP

Lexicon
  Det → the | a
  N   → government | interest | rates | internet | voice | man | dog | telescope
  V   → raised | gives | saw
  P   → with
  Pro → everyone

Two rules matter for sentence 3. VP → V NP PP lets the prepositional phrase attach to the verb phrase, giving the instrument reading, while NP → NP PP lets it attach inside the object noun phrase, giving the modifier reading. Both are in the grammar, so both derivations are legal and the grammar itself offers no way to choose between them.

Sentence 1

The government raised interest rates.

Sentence 2

The internet gives everyone a voice.

Sentence 3 — a structural ambiguity

The man saw the dog with the telescope.

This sentence has two grammatically valid parses, differing only in where the prepositional phrase attaches. The mauve branch marks the attachment point in each reading.

Labelled bracket notation

The same four analyses in the standard compact form.

(S (NP (Det The) (N government))
   (VP (V raised)
       (NP (N interest) (N rates))))

(S (NP (Det The) (N internet))
   (VP (V gives)
       (NP (Pro everyone))
       (NP (Det a) (N voice))))

Reading A — instrument (PP attached to VP):
(S (NP (Det The) (N man))
   (VP (V saw)
       (NP (Det the) (N dog))
       (PP (P with) (NP (Det the) (N telescope)))))

Reading B — modifier (PP attached to NP):
(S (NP (Det The) (N man))
   (VP (V saw)
       (NP (NP (Det the) (N dog))
           (PP (P with) (NP (Det the) (N telescope))))))

Discussion

Sentences 1 and 2 have a single well-formed analysis each, and the interest lies in what the tree records. In sentence 1, interest rates is a compound noun rather than two separate constituents: the grammar could in principle read raised interest as verb plus object and leave rates stranded, but no rule in the grammar rules that out. In sentence 2, gives subcategorises for two objects, so both NPs attach at the same level inside the VP — the tree encodes the verb's argument structure, not just word order.

Sentence 3 is the instructive case. Both parses are licensed by the same grammar, so the choice between them cannot be made syntactically. Swapping one word makes this plain: in the man saw the dog with the bone the modifier reading is obviously right, and in the man saw the dog with the telescope the instrument reading is preferred — yet the trees are structurally identical in each pair. What changes is knowledge about telescopes and bones, not grammar.

This is why practical parsers do not rely on grammar alone. Statistical parsers learn attachment preferences from annotated corpora, and neural models resolve attachment implicitly from context (Russell and Norvig, 2021). Both replace an absent rule with evidence about how language is actually used.

Critical evaluation

A context-free grammar of this kind overgenerates. The rules above will happily derive the telescope saw the government, which is grammatical and meaningless; nothing in the formalism encodes selectional restrictions on what can see. The grammar also says nothing about which of two valid parses is intended, and the problem grows quickly: with each additional prepositional phrase the number of attachment possibilities grows at the rate of the Catalan numbers, so a sentence with four PPs has dozens of valid parses (Church and Patil, 1982). Syntactic coverage and useful analysis are not the same thing.

Working through these trees also exposed how far the constituency template is built around English. It assumes a fixed subject–verb–object order and marks grammatical relations by position in the tree. Arabic, my first language, is verb-initial in its classical order and marks subject and object through case rather than position, so the same S → NP VP skeleton fits awkwardly and the NP–VP division carries less of the information. Dependency representations, which link words directly by grammatical relation rather than grouping them into phrases, travel between languages more comfortably for exactly that reason (Kübler, McDonald and Nivre, 2009). In a dependency tree every token is reachable by a single path from the root, and the edge labels carry the grammatical relation directly, which is what makes them a practical basis for rule-based analysis (Zimmerman, 2019).

Conclusion

The four trees show what a constituency analysis can and cannot deliver. It makes structure explicit — compound nouns, ditransitive argument structure, the two places a prepositional phrase can attach — and in doing so it locates ambiguity precisely, which is a genuine result rather than a failure. What it cannot do is choose between two grammatical readings, because that choice rests on meaning.

For an agent that takes instructions in natural language, the gap between grammaticality and meaning is the point at which parsing stops being a syntactic problem and becomes a question of what the agent knows about its domain. That is the same boundary the ontology draws in the KQML dialogue elsewhere in this module: a message can be perfectly well formed and still not be understood.
